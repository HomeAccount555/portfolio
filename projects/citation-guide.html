Citation Guide: Presentation & Poster Series

A five-poster series and a live presentation that teaches students how to cite sources properly, organized around the "4 Cs": Context, Conveyance, Connection and Citation. My part was the poster on quotations and the presentation sections on quotations and patchwriting.

Skills
Visual communication, information design, presenting
Tools
Canva
Project type
Course project, IAT 103W Design Communication & Collaboration
Timeline
May – August 2026
Team
5 students
My role
Quotations poster; presented quotations and patchwriting

The problem

Most students know they're supposed to cite, but the details are where they slip: when to use quotation marks, how long a quote can be before it needs its own block, and where paraphrasing ends and copying begins. Our team's job was to explain citation to other students in a way that is quick to take in, both on a wall and in a short talk.

How we worked

We split the topic into the 4 Cs and gave each person a poster, so every poster could stand on its own while the series covered the whole topic. We met every week: online to plan and divide the work, and in person to build and review the posters together.

My poster: All About Quotations

I took on quotations. I wanted the poster to answer the questions a student actually has, in the order they come up, so I split it into four parts:

  • Plagiarism: what counts as plagiarism with quotations, using the definition from our course module. Copying a paragraph directly is plagiarism, and so is citing the source but leaving out the quotation marks.
  • How to use quotations: put quotes under 40 words in quotation marks, and make anything longer its own block, each with an example.
  • Citing quotations: put the author's name, year and page number in brackets after the quote.
  • Common mistakes: either quote exactly or summarize, because anything in between is patchwriting. Save quotes for exact details like statistics.

For the look, I designed the poster like an old computer desktop. Each rule sits in its own pop-up window, and pixel-art pointers lead the eye from one window to the next. The theme makes a dry topic feel playful, and the windows split a lot of text into small, separate pieces. I closed with one line people would remember: "Bottom Line: If You're Going to Boldly Copy From Someone Directly, You Better Cite It!"

The presentation

In our group presentation I covered quotations and patchwriting. Patchwriting is the hardest idea to get across: it's when you keep someone else's sentence structure and only swap in a few words. It feels like paraphrasing, but it isn't. I explained it as the "in between" zone from my poster: a real summary uses your own words and structure, a quote uses theirs exactly, and anything in the middle needs to be pushed one way or the other.

Challenges

Too much text for one poster. My first list of rules was far longer than an A4 poster can hold and still be read from a few steps away. I cut it down to the rules students actually get wrong and gave each one its own window, so a reader can take in one idea at a time instead of facing a wall of text.

Making a dry topic inviting. Citation rules are nobody's favourite reading. The retro desktop theme, the pointers and the short bottom line were all ways to get people to stop and read the poster at all.

Five people, one series. Each of us owned a different C, so we used our in-person sessions to review the posters side by side and make sure they didn't repeat or contradict each other.

Outcome

Our team finished a five-poster series on the 4 Cs and presented it together. My poster covers the full quotation workflow, from what counts as plagiarism to how to cite correctly, on a single page.

Reflection

This project taught me that designing for a quick read is mostly about deciding what to leave out. The hardest part wasn't the visuals; it was cutting rules I knew were true but that would have crowded the page. I also learned how much a clear visual theme can do: the desktop windows gave the content a structure as well as a style.

If I did it again, I would replace the placeholder "lorem ipsum" text in my example windows with a real quote and citation, so readers could see the rule in action. I would also check the poster from a few metres away earlier, since some of the smaller pixel text is hard to read at a distance.
